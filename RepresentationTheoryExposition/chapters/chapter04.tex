
\section{Definitions}

Now, as we are done talking about the basics of representation theory, we get to the main goal of this exposition: introducing Semisimple algebras. The main claim we aim to prove in this chapter is that all semisimple algebras are product of matrix algebras. This characterization shows that semisimple algebras are the easiest to work with.

\begin{definition}[Semisimple Algebra]
    An algebra $A$ is \emph{semisimple} if when viewed as a representation of $A$ is a direct sum of irreducible representations of $A$.
    
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}
    A completely reducible representation of an alegbra $A$ is a direct sum of irreducible representations.
\end{definition}
There are many ways to define semisimple algebras, and they are all equivalent. At the end, we see that all of these definitions are just stating properties that such an algebra has.
We will prove that all of these properties are equivalent.

In fact, semisimple algebras are nice to work with since they are all isomorphic to a direct sum of matrix algebra (Artin-Wedderburn). Moreover, we will see a characterization for an arbitrary group algebra over a field (with some restrictions on the characterstic of that field) to be semisimple thus nice, and this is the main title of this exposition which is Maschke's Theorem.

Now, we have a nice lemma about irreducible representations that makes a lot of sense if we can see that the kernel and the image of an intertwining operator are subrepresentations.

\begin{lemma}[Schur's Lemma]
    Let $A$ be a $k-$algebra and $V,W$ be two irreducible representations of $A$. Let $\varphi: V \to W$ be an intertwining operator. Then $\varphi$ is either zero or an isomorphism. 
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Since $\ker \varphi$ is a subrepresentation of $V$ then $\ker V ={0}$ or $\ker V = V$. 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Case 1: If $\ker V =0 $, then $\varphi$ is injective. But also $W$ is irreducible so $\operatorname{Img} \varphi = {0}$ or $\operatorname{Img} \varphi = W$ by rank-nullity we get that $\operatorname{Img} \varphi $ must be $W$ hence $\varphi$ is an isomorphism.
        \item Case 2: if $\ker V = V$ then $\varphi$ is trivially the zero map. 
    \end{enumerate}
    and this ends the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Schur's Lemma for algebraically closed fields]
    Let $A$ be any $k-$algebra, and $V$ an irreducible representation of $A$. Then,
    \begin{align*}
        \operatorname{End}_A (V) \cong k \cdot id \cong k
    \end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Let $T \in \operatorname{End}_A(V)$ be any intertwining operator. Since $k$ is algebraically closed then $T$ has at least one eigenvalue in $k$. So, there is a non-zero vector $v \in V$ such that
    \begin{align*}
        T(v) = \lambda v
    \end{align*}
    for some $\lambda \in k$. From this equation we get that $T -\lambda \operatorname{id} (v) = 0 $. Since $T -\lambda \operatorname{id} \in \operatorname{End}_A(V)$ and $V$ is irreducible then it is either zero or an isomorphism. But $v$ is a non-zero vector in $\ker\big(T -\lambda \operatorname{id}\big) $. Therefore $T -\lambda \operatorname{id} = 0$, where $0$ is the zero map, and so $T = \lambda \operatorname{id}$. This completes the proof.
\end{proof}
Before we go to the main theorems we define the regular and trivial representations

\begin{definition}[Regular Representations]
    Let $A$ be any $k-$algebra. The regular representation of $A$ is the vector space structure of $A$, with the action on itself given by the multiplication operation defined on $A$ as an algebra.
More precisely, the pair $(A, \rho)$ is the regular representation of an algebra $A$, where $\rho: A \to \operatorname{End}(A)$ is given by the multiplication operation where:
\begin{align*}
    a \cdot b = \rho(a)(b) = ab
\end{align*}
for all $a,b \in A$
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Trivial Representation]
    Let $A = \operatorname{Mat}_d(\mathbb{R})$ be an algebra. The \emph{trivial representation} of $A$ is the vector space $V = k^{\oplus d}$, where the action of $A$ is given by regular matrix multiplication.

\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Radical]
    The radical of a finite dimensional algebra $A$ denoted by $\operatorname{Rad}(A)$ is an ideal of $A$ containing all elements that are mapped to the zero map in all irreducible representations of $A$. More precisely,
    \begin{align*}
        \operatorname{Rad}(A) = \bigcap_i \ker(\rho_i)
    \end{align*}
    where $(V_i, \rho_i)$ are the irreducible representations of $A$.
\end{definition}
We will then prove that the radical of $A$ is a two sided ideal

\begin{proof}
    Let $(V, \rho)$ be any irreducible representation of $A$. Pick any element $a \in A$ and an element $r \in \operatorname{Rad}(A)$. Then $\rho(r)(v) = 0$ for any $v \in V$. Since $\rho$ is a homomorphism of algebras, we get that
    \begin{align*}
       \rho(ar)(v) = \rho(a)(\rho(r)(v)) = \rho(a)(0) = 0
    \end{align*}
So, $ar \in \operatorname{Rad}(A)$. For the right side, since $r \in \operatorname{Rad}(A)$ then:
\begin{align*}
    \rho(ra)(v) = \rho(r)(\rho(a)(v)) = 0
\end{align*}
Thus, $ra \in \operatorname{Rad}(A)$, showing that $\operatorname{Rad}(A)$ is a two sided ideal
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The irreducible representations of a matrix algebra]
    Let $A = \operatorname{Mat}_d(k)$ be a matrix algebra. The only irreducible representation of $A$ is the trivial representation $V = k^{\oplus d}$. Moreover, any finite dimensional representation $W$ of $A$ is a direct sum of $k^{\oplus d}$ i.e. $W = V^{\oplus m}$ for some $m$.

\section{Density Theorem}
Before we introduce the density theorem we define the notation of a direct sum of functions denoted by $\displaystyle \bigoplus^r_{i =1} \rho_i$.
\begin{definition}\label{Def 3.10}
For any set $A$ and a collection of $r$ many sets $B_i$, where $1 \leq i \leq r$. Define $r$ many functions $\phi_i: A \to B_i$, then the \emph{direct sum} of $\phi_i$ is the function $\displaystyle \bigoplus^r_{i=1} \phi_i$ where:
\begin{align*}
    \bigoplus_{i= 1}^r \phi_i: A \to \bigoplus_{i=1}^r B_i,
\end{align*}
given by
\begin{align*}
    a \mapsto (\phi_1(a), \phi_2(a), \dots, \phi_r(a))
\end{align*}
or in a more compact notation $a \mapsto \bigoplus_{i=1}^r \phi_i(a)$ for every $a \in A$.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[Density Theorem]\label{3.11}
    Let $A$ be a finite dimensional algebra, and $V_1, V_2, \dots, V_r$ be the irreducible representations of $A$, then
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item For any $i$ we have that $\rho_i: A \to \operatorname{End}(V_i)$ is surjective
        \item The direct sum map $\bigoplus_{i=1}^m \rho_i: A \to \bigoplus_{i=1}^m \operatorname{End}(V_i)$ is surjective
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
So, from definition~\ref{Def 3.10} we can see that the image of $A$ in $\bigoplus_{i=1}^m \operatorname{End}(V_i)$ is a subset of the direct sum of the images of each $\rho_i$. To be more precise, we define the following sets for our proof:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The image of $A$ in $\bigoplus_{i=1}^m \operatorname{End}(V_i)$ is the set $B = \big \{\bigoplus_{i=1}^m \rho_i(a) \mid a \in A \big\}.$
    \item  The sets $B_i = \operatorname{Img} (\rho_i)$. Their direct sum $\bigoplus_{i=1}^m B_i = \big\{\bigoplus_{i=1}^m \rho_i(a_i) \mid a_i \in A \big\}$
\end{enumerate}
\begin{note}
\\
Those two sets $B$ and $\bigoplus_i B_i$ seem to be equal at the first glance, but in fact $B \subseteq \bigoplus^m_{i=1} B_i$. This is because the tuples in $B$ have the same $a$ as an input for each $\rho_i$, while in the direct sum $\bigoplus^m_{i=1} B_i$ it is not necessarily the case that the input to each $\rho_i$ is the same.
Now, what we will try to prove and what actually the theorem is telling us is that no matter what tuple of matrices you choose in $\bigoplus_{i=1}^m \operatorname{End}(V_i)$, or for any tuple with any combination of inputs to $\rho_i$, we will be able to find an input element $a \in A$
 such that the tuple we chose is exactly $\displaystyle \bigoplus_{i=1}^m \rho_i(a)$ (note that it is the same $a$!).
  This is what surjectivity actually mean here, we pick a tuple in $\bigoplus_{i=1}^m \operatorname{End}(V_i)$, which turns out to be equal to the direct sum $\bigoplus_i B_i$,
   and we want to show that this tuple is in the image of $A$ under the function $\bigoplus_{i=1}^m \rho_i$.

\end{note}
 We introduce the following two lemmas in our proof
   \begin{lemma}
    \label{lemma 3.12}
    Let $V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^m V_i^{\oplus n_i}$ where $V_i$ are pairwise non-isomorphic representations. 
    Then any subrepresentation of $V$ is of the form $W = \bigoplus_{i=1}^m V_i^{\oplus m_i}$ where $m_i \leq n_i$ for every $i$, and the inclusion map $\phi: W \hookrightarrow V$ is given by  a direct sum of inclusions of each summand $\phi_i: V_i^{\oplus n_i} \hookrightarrow V_i^{\oplus m_i}$, which are $n_i \times m_i$ matrices.
 \end{lemma}

 The intuition behind this lemma comes from Schur's lemma. \marginpar{To be continued}
   \begin{lemma}
    \label{lemma 3.13}
     Let $V$ be an irreducible representation of $A$ and consider any set of linearly independent vectors $v_1,v_2, \dots , v_r \in V$. 
     Then for any vectors $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_r \in V$, there is $a \in A$ such that $av_i = w_i$ for every $i$.

   \end{lemma}
   This lemma basically says that any set of vectors are mapped via some element in $A$ to a linearly independent set of vectors.
   \begin{proof}
   Let $V$ be an irreducible representation of dimension $n$ and $v_1, v_2, \dots v_n \in V$ be a linearly independent set. Consider the map $\phi: A \to V^{\oplus n}$ given by
   \begin{align*}
    a \mapsto (av_1, av_2, \dots, av_n)
   \end{align*} 
   . Assume for the sake of contradiction that there are vectors $w_1,w_2, \dots, w_n$ such that for any $a \in A$ we can't have $av_i = w_i$. Therefore, vectors $w_i \not \in \operatorname{Img}(\phi)$. So, the image of $\phi$ is a proper subrepresentation of $V^{\oplus n}$. By Lemma~\ref{lemma 3.12} $\operatorname{Img}(\phi) $ is of the form $V^{\oplus d}$ where $d < n$. 
   Since the map of inclusion $\operatorname{Img}(\phi) \to V^{\oplus n}$ is given by some $d \times n$ matrix $X$, then by rank-nullity we can find a nonzero vector of scalars $c = [c_1,c_2, \dots ,c_n]$ such that $X\textbf{c} = 0$. Now, consider the sum 
   \begin{align}
   \sum_i c_iv_i = [c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n] \begin{bmatrix}
    v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n 
   \end{bmatrix}
   \end{align}
 Note that $\phi(1) = (v_1,v_2, \dots , v_n)$, so $(v_1,v_2, \dots, v_n) \in \operatorname{Img}(\phi)$. Thus \begin{align*}
 [v_1,v_2 , \dots, v_n]^T =[q_1,q_2, \dots, q_d] X
 \end{align*} Substituting for $\textbf{v}^T$ in (3.1) we get:
 \begin{align*}
  \sum_i c_iv_i =  [q_1,q_2, \dots q_d] X [c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n]^T = \boldsymbol{0}\times [q_1,q_2, \dots, q_d] = 0
 \end{align*}
 contradicting that $v_i$ are linearly independent. This completes the proof.
\end{proof}
\begin{lemma}
    Let $A$ be a finite dimensional algebra. Let $(V, \rho)$ be a representation of $A$. Consider $\operatorname{Mat(V)}$ as a representation of $A$ with the action given by matrix multiplication:
    \begin{align*}
        a \cdot M = \rho(a) \cdot M.
    \end{align*} 
    Then the following is true:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The map $\rho: A \to \operatorname{Mat(V)}$ is an intertwining operator, where $A$ is the regular representation.
        \item We have the following isomorphism of representations $\operatorname{Mat}(V) \cong V^{\oplus d}$, where $d$ is the dimension of $V$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    First we prove $(1)$. Let $A$ and $\operatorname{Mat}(V)$ be as before. To show that $\rho$ is intertwining we will show that for any $a,b \in A$ we have that $\rho(a b) = a  \cdot \rho(b)$, where $ab$ is a product in $A$ and $\cdot $ is the action of $a$ on $\rho(b) \in \operatorname{Mat}(V)$. By definition of $V$ as $A-$module we have that $\rho(ab) = \rho(a) \cdot \rho(b)$. By definition of the action of $A$, where $a \cdot M = \rho(a) \cdot M$ for all $a \in A$ and $M \in \operatorname{Mat}(V)$, we get the desired equality 
    \begin{align*}
        \rho(ab) = \rho(a) \cdot \rho(b) = a \cdot \rho(b).
    \end{align*}
For $2$, consider the map $\phi: \operatorname{Mat}(V) \to V^{\oplus d}$ given by mapping every matrix in $\operatorname{Mat}(V)$ to the vector in $V^{\oplus d}$ with the $i-$th entry representing the $i-$th column in the matrix. 
That is 
\begin{align*}
   \begin{pmatrix}
     m_{11} & m_{12} & \dots & m_{1d} \\
    m_{21} & m_{22} & \dots & m_{2d}\\
    \vdots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    m_{d1} & m_{d2} & \dots & m_{dd}
\end{pmatrix} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 
         m_{11} \\
         m_{21} \\
         \vdots \\
         m_{d1} 
    \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} m_{12} \\ m_{22} \\ \vdots \\ m_{d2}\end{bmatrix}, \dots , \begin{bmatrix}
        m_{1d} \\ m_{2d} \\ \vdots \\ m_{dd} 
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
This map is clearly surjective. For injectivity suppose for any $M_1, M_2 \in \operatorname{Mat}(V)$ we have $\phi(M_1) = \phi(M_2)$, then the columns of the two matrices are equal so $M_1 = M_2$. It remains to show that $\phi$ is intertwining that is $\phi(a \cdot M) = a \cdot \phi(M)$. By definition of action of $a$ on $M$ we get 
\begin{align*}
    \phi(a \cdot M) = \phi(\rho(a) \cdot M)
\end{align*}
By definition of matrix multiplication, it works on columns independently. Let 

\begin{align*}
\rho(a) = \begin{pmatrix}
    a_{11} & a_{12} & \dots & a_{1d} \\
    a_{21} & a_{22} & \dots & a_{2d}\\
    \vdots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    a_{d1} & a_{d2} & \dots & a_{dd}
\end{pmatrix} , \quad M = \begin{pmatrix}
     m_{11} & m_{12} & \dots & m_{1d} \\
    m_{21} & m_{22} & \dots & m_{2d}\\
    \vdots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    m_{d1} & m_{d2} & \dots & m_{dd}
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
We can write $\rho(a)$ and $M$ in a more compact notation  $a = \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathbf{a_1} \\
    \mathbf{a_2} \\
    \vdots \\
    \mathbf{a_d}
\end{pmatrix} \quad \text{, where $\mathbf{a_i}$ is the $i-$th row}.$ \\
\begin{align*} M = \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathbf{c_1} & \mathbf{c_2} & \dots & \mathbf{c_d}
\end{pmatrix} &\quad \text{, where $\mathbf{c_j}$ is the $j-$th column}. 
\end{align*}
So, the product $\rho(a) \cdot M$ is 
\begin{align*}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \mathbf{a_1 c_1} & \mathbf{a_1 c_2} & \dots& \mathbf{a_1 c_d} \\
        \mathbf{a_2 c_1} & \mathbf{a_2 c_2} & \dots &\mathbf{a_2 c_d} \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        \mathbf{a_d c_1} & \mathbf{a_d c_2} & \dots  & \mathbf{a_d c_d}
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
The $i-$th column of the product could be seen as $(d \times 1)-$matrix resulting from multiplying the matrix $a_{d \times d}$ with the $i-$th column of the matrix $M_{d \times 1}$. So, the $i-$th column of the product is exactly the $i-$th entry of the vector $a \cdot \phi(M)$, where $a$ acts on this vector by matrix multiplication with each entry as follows:
\begin{align*}
    a \cdot \phi(M) =  \begin{pmatrix}
   a \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 
         m_{11} \\
         m_{21} \\
         \vdots \\
         m_{d1} 
    \end{bmatrix}, a \cdot \begin{bmatrix} m_{12} \\ m_{22} \\ \vdots \\ m_{d2}\end{bmatrix}, \dots ,  a \cdot \begin{bmatrix}
        m_{1d} \\ m_{2d} \\ \vdots \\ m_{dd} 
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
Therefore $\phi(a \cdot M) = a \cdot \phi(M)$ as desired.
\end{proof}
Now, we are ready to prove the density theorem

\begin{proof}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Since $V_i$ is a representation of $A$, then $\operatorname{End}(V_i) \cong \operatorname{Mat}(V_i) \cong V_i^{\oplus d_i}$, where $d_i$ is the dimension of $V_i$. Since $\operatorname{Img}(\rho_i)$ is a subrepresentation of $\operatorname{End}(V_i)$ then by lemma~\ref{lemma 3.12}, $\operatorname{Img}(\rho_i)$ is of the form $V^{\oplus m}$ for some $m \leq d$. We will show that $m = d$. Let $v_1, v_2, \dots , v_d$ be a basis of $V^{\oplus d}$. By linear algebra we know that the columns of the any matrix in $\operatorname{Mat}(V)$ is just the images of the basis vector. 
        So, via $\rho_i$ and the isomorphism $\operatorname{End}(V_i) \cong \operatorname{Mat}(V_i) \cong  V^{\oplus d}$,
        we get $a \mapsto (av_1, av_2 , \dots, av_n)$. This is exactly the image of $A$ in $\operatorname{End}(V_i)$, because the matrix $\rho(a)$ is completely determined by where it sends the basis vectors of $V$. 
        Pick any vector $(w_1,w_2, \dots, w_d) \in V^{\oplus d}$, where each $w_i \in V$. By lemma~\ref{lemma 3.13}, there is $a \in A$ such that $(w_1, w_2 , \dots , w_d) = (av_1, av_2, \dots , av_d)$. Therefore, $(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_d) \in \operatorname{Img}(\rho_i) = V^{\oplus m}$, then $V^{\oplus d} \subseteq V^{\oplus m}$, and $m =d$ yielding surjectivity as desired.
    
\item For the case of $r \geq 2$, we get that the image of the direct sum $\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i =1}^r \rho_i$ is a subrepresentation of $\bigoplus_{i=1}^r \operatorname{End}(V_i) \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^r V_i^{\oplus d_i}$. By lemma~\ref{lemma 3.12}, the image is of the form $\bigoplus_{i=1}^r V_i^{\oplus m_i}$, and the inclusion is a direct sum of the inclusions of each summand. By $(1)$, we have shown that the inclusion of each summand is surjective. Therefore, the map $\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i =1}^r \rho_i$ is surjective.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}
  
\begin{corollary}[Finitely many irreps]
    Let $A$ be any finite dimensional algebra, then $A$ has finitely many finite dimensional irreducible representations $V_i$. Moreover, we have the following isomorphism of algebras:
    \begin{align*}
    \bigslant{A}{\operatorname{Rad}(A)} \cong \bigoplus_{i} \operatorname{End}(V_i)
    \end{align*}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    Let $A$ be as above. Consider $V$ an irreducible representation of $A$. Then for any non-zero $v \in V$ we have that $Av$ is a subrepresentation (a subspace closed under the action of $A$). Since $Av \neq 0$ and $V$ is irreducible then $Av = V$. Since $A$ is finite dimensional then $V$ is finite dimensional. Since the irreducible representations we considered in Theorem~\ref{3.11} are pairwise non isomorphic, we get that $\sum_{i =1}^{r} \dim(V)^2 = \dim(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r} \operatorname{End}(V_i))$. By surjectivity of the map $\rho$ in Theorem~\ref{3.11}, and rank-nullity theorem we get that $\sum_{i =1}^{r} \dim(V_i)^2 \leq \dim(A)$. Since $\dim(V_i) \geq 1$ then $r \leq  \sum_{i =1}^{r} \dim(V_i)^2 \leq \dim(A)$, where $r$ is the number of the irreducible representations of $A$, yielding that they are finite as desired. 
   
    For the moreover part, clearly $\operatorname{Rad}(A) \subseteq \ker(\rho)$. For the other inclusion, let $a \in A$ be an element of $\ker(\rho)$. Then $\rho(a) = (0,0, \dots , 0)$, where each entry is $\rho_i(a)$. This shows that $a$ is mapped to the zero map in every irreducible representation. Thus $a \in \operatorname{Rad}(A)$. So, $\ker(\rho) \subseteq \operatorname{Rad}(A)$. By the universal property of quotients the map $\rho$ factors through $\operatorname{Img}(\rho)$. Since $\rho$ is surjective then $\operatorname{Img}(\rho) = \bigoplus_{i} \operatorname{End}(V_i)$ showing the desired isomorphism.
\end{proof}
\section{Maschke's Theorem}

   \begin{theorem}[Maschke Theorem]
    Let $G$ be a finite group, and $k$ be a field where $\operatorname{char}(k) \nmid |G|$. Let $(V_i, \rho_i)$ be the irreducible representations of $G$ for $ 1 \leq i \leq r$, then
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $k[G]$ is semi-simple.
        \item $\phi: k[G] \to \displaystyle \bigoplus_{i =1}^{r} \operatorname{End}(V_i)$ is an isomorphism of algebras.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

First, we introduce the definition of an idempotent map.
\begin{definition}
    Let $V$ be a vector space. Let $P: V \to V$ be a linear map. We say $P$ is idempotent if $P^2 = P$ for all $v \in V$.
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}
       Let $P$ be as before, then $P$ is the identity map on its image.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    Since $P$ is idempotent then $P^2 = P$ for any $v \in V$. Consider $u \in \operatorname{img}(P)$, then $u = P(v)$ for some $v \in V$. Thus, 
    \begin{align*}
        P(u)  = P(P(v)) =  P(v)  = u.
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}
We use the following lemmas in the proof.

\begin{lemma}
    For any idempotent map $P:V \to V$, we have that 
    \begin{align*}
        V = \operatorname{Img}(P) \oplus \operatorname{ker}(P).
    \end{align*}
    as vector spaces. 
\end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}
   Let $U,W$ be any subspaces of $V$. Suppose $V = U \oplus W$, then $U = \operatorname{Img}(P) $ and $W = \operatorname{ker}(P)$ for some idempotent map $P$.
\end{lemma}
This lemma shows that any decomposition of $V$ into a direct sum is given by some idempotent map in the background.

\begin{proof}
    Consider $P$ the projection along $W$ onto $U$ that is for any $v = (u ,w) \in V$, where $u \in U$ and $w \in W$, we have that 
    $P((u,w)) = (u,0)$. 
    then $P^2 = P$ for every $v \in V$. By definition of $P$ we get $\operatorname{Img}(P) \cong U$ and $\ker(P) \cong W $ because a vector $v$ in the kernel is mapped to zero, which means its $u$ coordinate is $0$ i.e. of the form $(0,w)$. Clearly, $P$ is idempotent, because
    \begin{align}
        P(P((u,w))) = P((u,0)) = (u,0),
    \end{align}
    Observe that the first equality in (3.2) holds because $\operatorname{Img}(P) = U$, and the second holds because $P\mid_U = \operatorname{id}$.

\end{proof}

Now, we are ready to prove Maschke's Theorem.
\begin{proof}

    First, suppose $(V, \rho)$ is a representation of $G$, and $W \subset V$ is a subrepresentation of $V$. We will show that there is $W^\prime$ a subrepresentation of $V$ such that $V = W \oplus W^\prime$ as representations.
    Consider the projection $P: V \to W$ onto $W$. So, $\operatorname{Img}(P) = W$, and $P\mid_W = \operatorname{id}$. We will construct a subrepresentation $W^\prime$ such that $V = W \oplus W^\prime$.

    \subsubsection*{Step 1: $\operatorname{Img}(\overline{P}) = W$}
    Consider the average map $\overline{P}: V \to V$ defined as
    \begin{align*}
        \overline{P} := \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g) P \rho(g^{-1}) 
    \end{align*}
    Let $\ker(\overline{P}) = W^\prime$. Pick any $v \in V$, then 
    \begin{align*}
            \overline{P}(v) &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g)\big( P \big(\rho(g^{-1})(v)\big)\big) \\
                            &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g)\big( P(v^\prime)\big) \\
                            &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g)(w)\\
                            &=\frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} w^\prime = \frac{1}{|G|}\times|G| \cdot w^\prime = w^\prime\in W.
    \end{align*}
    So, $\operatorname{Img}(\overline{P}) \subseteq W$. To show the other inclusion, we will show that $\overline{P}$ is the identity on $W$. Pick any $w \in W$
    \begin{align*}
        \overline{P}(w) &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g)\big( P \big(\rho(g^{-1})(w)\big)\big) \\
                            &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g)\big( \rho(g^{-1} w)\big) \\
                            &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} w = \frac{1}{|G|}\times|G| \cdot w = w.
    \end{align*}
    Thus, $\operatorname{Img}(\overline{P}) = W$. Pick any $v \in V$, the image $\overline{P}(v) = w$ for some $w \in W$, then
    \begin{align*}
         \overline{P}(\overline{P}(v)) = \overline{P}(w) = w = P(v)
    \end{align*}
    So, $\overline{P}$ is idempotent. Hence, $V = \operatorname{Img}(\overline{P}) \oplus \ker(\overline{P}) = W \oplus W^\prime$ as vector spaces. We know $W$ is a subrepresentation. It remains to show that $W^\prime = \ker(\overline{P})$ is a subrepresentation.
   \\ 
   \subsubsection*{Step 2: \text{$\overline{P}$ is intertwining}} 
Pick any $h \in G$ and any $v \in V$, we will show that $\overline{P}(\rho(h)v) = \rho(h) \overline{P}(v)$.
\begin{align*}
    \overline{P}(\rho(h) v) &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g)\big( P \big(\rho(g^{-1})( \rho(h)(v))\big)\big) \\
                            &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g)\big( P \big(   \rho(g^{-1} h)(v)  \big) \big)
\end{align*}
Let $l^{-1} = g^{-1} h$. So, $l = (g^{-1} h)^{-1} = h^{-1} g$ Then $g = hl$. Substitute in the last equation
\begin{align*}
    \overline{P}(\rho(h) v) &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{l \in G} \rho(hl)\big( P \big(   \rho(l^{-1})(v)  \big) \big) \\
                            &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{l \in G} \rho(h) \rho(l)\big( P \big(   \rho(l^{-1})(v)  \big) \big) \\
                            &= \rho(h) \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{l \in G} \rho(h) \rho(l)\big( P \big(   \rho(l^{-1})(v)  \big) \big) \\
                            &= \rho(h) \overline{P}(v).
                        \end{align*}                
                        as desired. Since $\overline{P}$ is intertwining, then $\ker(\overline{P})$ is a subrepresentation. Therefore, $V = W \oplus W^\prime$ as representations.
    % $\overline{P}$ is the identity on W, kernel of $\overline{P}$ is $W^\prime$ and its image is the same as $P$, then $V = \operatorname{Img}(\overline{P}) \oplus \ker(\overline{P}) = W \oplus W^\prime$. Since $\overline{P}$ is intertwining then $\ker(\overline{P})$ is a subrepresentation.
    \subsubsection*{Step 3}
    We have shown that if $V$ is a representation and $W \subset V$ is a subrepresentation then $V = W \oplus W^\prime$ for some subrepresentation $W^\prime$. Now, we will show by induction on $\dim(V)$, under the same hypotheses, that $V$ is a direct sum of irreducible representations.
    If $\dim(V)= 1$ then it is immediate that $V$ is irreducible. Let $n \geq 2$. Suppose that for any representation $V^\prime$ of $\dim \leq n$ is a direct sum of irreducible representations. Let $V$ be a representation with $\dim(V) = n +1$. By \textbf{Step 2}, $V = W \oplus W^\prime$, where $\dim(W) \leq n$, and $\dim(W^\prime) \leq n$. By the induction hypothesis $W,W^\prime$ are direct sums of irreducible representations, showing the claim is true.
    Since any representation $V$ of $G$ is completely reducible, then $k[G]$ is semi-simple as desired.             
\end{proof}

\begin{note}

    Observe that the proof of Maschke's theorem shows that, under the hypothesis of the theorem, $k[G]$ is semi-simple by
     showing that any representation of $k[G]$ is completely reducible (since they are equivalent as in theorem~\ref{theorem 3.8}). Therefore, any indecomposable representation is irreducible. One can easily show that this fails in general. 
     That is there are indecomposable representations of $k[G]$ that are not irreducible, if $\operatorname{char}(k)$ divides $|G|$.
\end{note}
Now, we give an example of an indecomposable representation of $G$ which is reducible. This happens if $\operatorname{char} (k) \mid |G|$.

\begin{corollary}[The sum of squares formula]
    Let $V_i$ be as above, then
    \begin{align*}
        |G| = \displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^r \dim(V_i)^2.
    \end{align*}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    The proof is immediate from Theorem~\ref{theorem 3.8} (2), and the fact that $\dim(k[G]) = |G|$.
\end{proof}
